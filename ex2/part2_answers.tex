\section*{Failure Type: Implicit Negative Sentiment}
% ============================================================

The failure type I investigated is \textbf{implicit negative sentiment}: sentences
whose true sentiment is negative, but which contain \emph{no explicit negative
vocabulary}. The negativity must be inferred from understatement, irony, or
real-world consequence rather than read off surface words.

Examples of this type of sentence include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{``This umbrella kept me perfectly dry for the first ten seconds of the storm.''}
  \item \emph{``The battery lasts just long enough to get from my bedroom to the kitchen.''}
  \item \emph{``I got to sample the entire menu, as each dish came back to the kitchen at least once.''}
\end{itemize}

None of these sentences contain words like \emph{bad}, \emph{disappointing}, or
\emph{terrible}. A reader understands the negative sentiment by reasoning about
what the stated facts \emph{imply} about the product or experience --- an umbrella
that lasts ten seconds in a storm is useless; a battery that only reaches the
kitchen is absurdly short; dishes returning to the kitchen means the food was sent
back. This kind of reasoning requires world knowledge and pragmatic inference, not
just lexical matching.

% ============================================================
\section*{Hypothesis}
% ============================================================

\textbf{Hypothesis:} Instruction-tuned language models performing zero-shot
sentiment classification over-rely on the presence of explicit positive or negative
vocabulary. When the surface words of a sentence are neutral or even positive
(``perfectly dry'', ``successfully assembled'', ``admirably''), the model defaults
to \texttt{Positive}, ignoring the pragmatic implication that the stated
performance is far below any reasonable expectation.

This is a form of \emph{lexical shortcut}: the model has learned that negative
words correlate strongly with negative sentiment and positive words with positive
sentiment during training, and it applies this heuristic without engaging in the
deeper inference required for understatement.

sentiment word. The negativity arises entirely from understatement or implied

\noindent
\textbf{Observation.} Qwen3-8B failed on 8 out of 9 examples, predicting
\texttt{Positive} in every case. The one exception (item~1) is the sentence
where the time qualifier (``ten seconds'') is perhaps most salient as an absurd
number. Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct failed on 6 out of 9, with two predictions of
\texttt{Neutral} (items~4 and~7). Both models comfortably exceed the threshold of
five failures, confirming that the failure type is real and reproducible.

% ============================================================
\section*{Hypothesis Tests}
% ============================================================

To test the hypothesis more precisely, I designed eight controlled inputs that
vary \emph{only the degree of explicitness} of the negativity, while keeping the
underlying scenario constant (an umbrella that fails quickly). This allows us to
isolate the effect of lexical explicitness on model predictions.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{H1--H2 (explicit negative, control):} fully explicit negative words present.
        Model should classify as \texttt{Negative}.
  \item \textbf{H3 (sarcasm marker):} the phrase ``Oh great'' signals irony.
        Model should classify as \texttt{Negative}.
  \item \textbf{H4--H6 (understatement / implied consequence):} zero explicit negative words.
        \emph{Predicted model failure}: should predict \texttt{Positive}.
  \item \textbf{H7--H8 (explicit positive, control):} clearly positive sentences.
        Model should classify as \texttt{Positive}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{table}[h!]
\centering
\caption{Hypothesis tests: controlled variation of lexical explicitness.}
\label{tab:hyp}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{c L{7cm} c c c}
\toprule
\# & Input & Gold & Qwen3-8B & Llama-3.1-8B \\
\midrule
H1 & This umbrella broke in the first minute of rain and I got completely soaked.
   & Neg & Neg & Neg \\
H2 & This umbrella is useless. It fell apart immediately and left me drenched.
   & Neg & Neg & Neg \\
H3 & Oh great, another umbrella that lasts all of ten seconds in the rain.
   & Neg & Neg & Neg \\
H4 & This umbrella kept me dry for the first ten seconds of the storm.
   & Neg & \textbf{Pos} & Neg \\
H5 & The battery lasts just long enough to reach the coffee machine from my desk.
   & Neg & Neg & Neg \\
H6 & I used this umbrella once before going back to my old one.
   & Neg & Neg & \textbf{Neu} \\
H7 & This umbrella is fantastic. It kept me completely dry through two hours of heavy rain.
   & Pos & Pos & Pos \\
H8 & This umbrella handled a two-hour downpour without a single leak.
   & Pos & Pos & Pos \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\noindent
\textbf{Discussion.} The hypothesis is \emph{partially confirmed}. Both models
correctly classify the explicit negative controls (H1--H2) and the sarcasm-marked
example (H3), showing that the failures on the break examples are not due to a
general inability to classify negatives. For the understatement probes, Qwen3-8B
failed on H4 (``ten seconds'') as predicted, but classified H5 and H6 correctly,
suggesting it can sometimes infer implication from highly specific numbers or
from the use of ``once.'' Llama-3.1-8B was more robust on H4--H5 but produced
\texttt{Neutral} on H6, indicating partial confusion on implied-consequence
sentences. Both models succeed on the positive controls H7--H8.

The pattern is consistent with the hypothesis: \emph{the more implicit the
negativity, the more likely the model is to fail}, even if the relationship is not
perfectly monotone on the controlled test set.

% ============================================================
\section*{Comparison Across Models}
% ============================================================

Qwen3-8B shows the sharper failure: it predicted \texttt{Positive} confidently on
8/9 break examples, often with \emph{``confident''} or \emph{``very confident''}
reported in its output. This suggests it has essentially no mechanism for inferring
negativity from understatement. Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct is somewhat more cautious
--- it sometimes returns \texttt{Neutral} instead of \texttt{Positive}, and it
correctly classified two examples that Qwen got wrong. However, it still failed on
6/9 examples, confirming the failure type is not model-specific.

% ============================================================
\section*{Proposed Solution}
% ============================================================

\textbf{Two-step prompting.} The core problem is that the model must do pragmatic
inference before it can classify sentiment. The proposed solution is to make this
inference explicit by splitting the task into two prompts:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Step 1 -- Implication extraction:} Ask the model to paraphrase
        what the sentence implies about the product or experience in plain terms.
        Prompt: \emph{``In one sentence, describe what the following review implies
        about the product's quality: [input].''}
  \item \textbf{Step 2 -- Classification:} Classify the \emph{generated paraphrase}
        from step~1, not the original sentence. Because the model's own paraphrase
        will contain explicit evaluative language (e.g.,~\emph{``The umbrella fails
        very quickly in rain''}), the standard lexical classifier works correctly.
\end{enumerate}

For example, given \emph{``This umbrella kept me perfectly dry for the first ten
seconds of the storm,''} step~1 would likely produce something like \emph{``The
umbrella provides almost no protection from rain.''} Classifying that paraphrase
yields \texttt{Negative} correctly.

This solution requires no additional training or labeled data and is compatible
with any instruction-tuned model. Its cost is one extra forward pass per input.
The potential downside is that the quality of step~2 depends on step~1: if the
model's paraphrase is itself vague or overly neutral, the classification may still
be wrong. Nonetheless, this approach directly addresses the root cause ---
the absence of explicit sentiment cues --- by generating them before classifying.
