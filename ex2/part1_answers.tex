\section*{Question 1 \;---\; Societal Bias Beyond Gender}
% ============================================================

In class we looked at gender bias as a prominent example of societal bias in NLP.
Below I describe a paper from the ACL Anthology that focuses on a different type
of societal bias: \textbf{stereotypical bias tied to race and religion}.

\medskip
\textbf{Paper:} Moin Nadeem, Anna Bethke, and Siva Reddy.
``StereoSet: Measuring stereotypical bias in pretrained language models.''
\emph{Proceedings of ACL-IJCNLP 2021}.

\medskip
\textbf{What the paper introduces (dataset).}
StereoSet is a large crowdsourced dataset built specifically to measure
how much pretrained language models rely on social stereotypes when predicting
text. The dataset contains roughly \textbf{17,000 items} across four bias
categories: race, religion, profession, and gender. Since Q1 asks about
non-gender bias, the relevant portions are the race and religion subsets, which
together cover around \textbf{400 unique target groups}
(e.g., specific racial backgrounds and world religions).

Each item consists of a short context followed by three candidate continuations:
a \emph{stereotypical} one, an \emph{anti-stereotypical} one, and a semantically
\emph{unrelated} (meaningless) one. For example, given the context
``\emph{Arabs are...},'' the three options might be
``violent and dangerous'' (stereotype), ``peaceful and welcoming'' (anti-stereotype),
and ``made of bricks'' (unrelated).

\textbf{Domain and collection.}
The data was collected from English-speaking crowdworkers on Amazon Mechanical Turk.
Items come in two structural formats: \emph{intrasentence}
(the model fills in a blank within a single sentence) and
\emph{intersentence} (the model continues a short passage), giving a broad
coverage of contexts in which bias can surface.

\textbf{How bias is quantified.}
The paper proposes two metrics: the \emph{Language Model Score} (LMS), which
measures whether the model generally prefers meaningful completions over nonsensical
ones (a proxy for language understanding), and the \emph{Stereotype Score} (SS),
which measures how often the model favors the stereotypical option over the
anti-stereotypical one. A perfectly unbiased model would score 50 on SS.
The two are combined into the \emph{ICAT score}, which rewards models that are
both linguistically capable and minimally biased. The authors find that all
tested models (BERT, RoBERTa, XLNet, GPT-2) show clear stereotypical preferences
in both the race and religion categories.

\section*{Question 2 \;---\; Three Efficiency Methods}
% ============================================================

In class we covered four categories of efficiency techniques: training,
inference, modeling, and data methods. I chose one method from three of these
categories.

% ------ Method 1 ------
\subsection*{Method 1: Quantization \quad\textnormal{\small(Inference efficiency)}}

\textbf{(a) Description.}
Quantization lowers the numerical precision of a model's weights and activations.
Instead of the standard 32-bit floating point (float32), weights are stored and
computed in float16, INT8, or even INT4. This can be done after training
(\emph{post-training quantization}) without any retraining, or during training
(\emph{quantization-aware training}) so the model learns to tolerate the
precision reduction.

\textbf{(b) Resources saved.}
Quantization primarily reduces \emph{memory}: a 7B-parameter model takes roughly
28\,GB in float32 but only about 3.5\,GB in INT4. It also speeds up
\emph{inference runtime}, since lower-precision arithmetic executes faster on
modern hardware. The savings are especially relevant for deployment on GPUs with
limited VRAM.

\textbf{(c) Trade-off.}
The resource savings come at the cost of a small drop in \emph{accuracy},
which becomes more noticeable at very aggressive quantization (e.g., 4-bit).
Quantization-aware training recovers most of the accuracy loss, but it requires
additional \emph{training compute} -- so there is a direct trade-off between
deployment efficiency and training cost.

% ------ Method 2 ------
\subsection*{Method 2: LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) \quad\textnormal{\small(Training efficiency)}}

\textbf{(a) Description.}
LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) is a parameter-efficient fine-tuning method. The idea is
to freeze all the pretrained weights and inject small \emph{low-rank} matrices
into each transformer layer. Only these adapter matrices are updated during
fine-tuning, while the rest of the model stays unchanged. Once training is done,
the adapters are merged back into the original weights, so inference cost is
identical to the base model.

\textbf{(b) Resources saved.}
LoRA reduces both \emph{training memory} and \emph{training compute} dramatically.
For a 7B model, the number of trainable parameters drops from about 7 billion
to roughly 20--30 million (under 0.5\%), making it possible to fine-tune
large models on a single consumer GPU.

\textbf{(c) Trade-off.}
The main cost is a slight reduction in \emph{expressiveness}: because the weight
updates are constrained to a low-rank subspace, LoRA may fall slightly behind
full fine-tuning on some tasks. In practice the gap is small. There is also a
minor overhead of managing separate adapter files when deploying the same base
model with multiple task-specific adapters.

% ------ Method 3 ------
\subsection*{Method 3: Knowledge Distillation \quad\textnormal{\small(Modeling efficiency)}}

\textbf{(a) Description.}
Knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 2015) trains a smaller \emph{student}
model to replicate the behavior of a larger \emph{teacher} model. Rather than
training the student on hard (one-hot) labels, it is trained to match the
teacher's full output distribution (``soft'' probabilities), which carries
richer information. A well-known NLP example is DistilBERT (Sanh et al., 2019),
a 66M-parameter student trained from BERT-base (110M parameters) that retains
about 97\% of BERT's performance on the GLUE benchmark.

\textbf{(b) Resources saved.}
The result is a smaller model that saves \emph{inference memory},
\emph{inference compute}, and \emph{latency}. DistilBERT, for instance,
is 40\% smaller than BERT-base and roughly 60\% faster at inference time.

\textbf{(c) Trade-off.}
The inference savings come at the expense of extra \emph{training cost}:
the distillation process requires running the large teacher to generate soft
labels and then training the student on them, which takes more time and compute
than training the student from scratch on hard labels alone. There is also a
performance ceiling -- the student is unlikely to match the teacher on complex
or rare inputs.

% ------ Pairwise combinations ------
\subsection*{Pairwise Combination Analysis}

\paragraph{Quantization + LoRA.}
These two methods are highly complementary and are already combined in practice
under the name \textbf{QLoRA} (Dettmers et al., 2023). The frozen base model is
quantized to 4-bit (saving the GPU memory needed to hold it), while the LoRA
adapters are still trained in 16-bit precision. This combination makes it
possible to fine-tune a 65B-parameter model on a single 48\,GB GPU -- something
neither method could achieve independently. There is no fundamental conflict
between them.

\paragraph{Quantization + Knowledge Distillation.}
These methods are also compatible and are commonly used in sequence. A natural
pipeline is: (1) distill a large teacher into a smaller student, and (2) apply
quantization to the student for deployment. The two steps independently reduce
the inference footprint, and their effects stack: a student that is 4$\times$
smaller than the teacher, then quantized to INT8, occupies about one-eighth the
memory of the original teacher. The only downside is two sequential training
phases rather than one.

\paragraph{LoRA + Knowledge Distillation.}
These methods target different stages of the model lifecycle -- LoRA reduces
fine-tuning cost, while distillation reduces model size -- so they address
different problems. They can be combined: for example, one could first distill
a large model into a smaller student, and then use LoRA to fine-tune that
student efficiently for a downstream task. This is useful when both
\emph{deployment efficiency} (small model) and \emph{training efficiency}
(cheap adaptation) are required at the same time. They are not mutually
exclusive.
